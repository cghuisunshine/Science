Inquiry Question

How do my genetic traits compare to those of others?

Introduction

Human traits are influenced by genetic information inherited from our biological parents, but the way traits appear in a population is often more complex than a simple dominant-versus-recessive pattern. Some easily observed traits, such as detached earlobes, freckles, tongue rolling, and handedness, are commonly used in introductory genetics because they allow students to compare variation within a group. By collecting data from multiple people, it becomes possible to see that individuals share some traits while differing in others, and that no single person has the exact same combination of traits as everyone else.

This project investigates how my own observable genetic traits compare with those of a sample group of 20 people. The purpose is to identify patterns in trait frequency, represent those patterns clearly in a data table and bar graph, and compare the class or family sample with approximate frequencies reported for the general population. This investigation connects genetics with data literacy because it requires careful observation, organized recording, graphical communication, and interpretation of numerical results. Overall, the project shows that inherited traits vary across groups and that population data can be used to place an individual's traits in a broader biological context.

Materials

  • Genetic trait survey form
  • Responses from 20 participants
  • Pencil or pen
  • Spreadsheet or graphing software such as Excel or Google Sheets, or graph paper and colored pencils

Method

  1. I reviewed the trait inventory provided in the assignment handout.
  2. I recorded my own observable genetic traits.
  3. I surveyed 20 people and marked whether each participant answered yes or no for each trait.
  4. I counted the total number of yes and no responses for every trait.
  5. I entered the totals into a summary data table.
  6. I created a bar graph showing the number of yes and no responses for each trait.
  7. I converted selected trait counts into percentages using the formula below.
  8. I compared my sample frequencies with the approximate frequencies listed in the assignment sheet for the general population.
  9. I interpreted the patterns and wrote a conclusion and reflection.

Formula

Frequency (%) = (Number of people with the trait / Total number of people surveyed) × 100

Example:

17 / 20 × 100 = 85%

Analysis

The survey results show that the traits were not distributed evenly across the group. The most common traits in the sample were the ability to see red and green (19 out of 20 people, or 95%), being right-handed (18 out of 20, or 90%), tongue rolling (15 out of 20, or 75%), and detached earlobes (14 out of 20, or 70%). In contrast, the least common traits were freckles (3 out of 20, or 15%), naturally curly hair (4 out of 20, or 20%), and cleft chin (5 out of 20, or 25%). The bar graph makes these differences easy to see because the tallest yes bars represent the most frequent traits, while the shortest bars show the least frequent ones.

When I compared my sample to the approximate general-population frequencies in the assignment handout, several of my results were very close. Left-thumb-on-top hand clasping in my group was exactly 55%, which matched the handout. Straight hairline was 40% in my group compared with 41% in the general population, cleft chin was 25% compared with 26%, detached earlobes were 70% compared with 72%, and red-green color vision was 95% compared with 95.5%. These close matches suggest that, for some traits, my sample group was fairly representative of the broader pattern.

Some traits showed larger differences. Freckles appeared in 15% of my group, which was higher than the 5% listed in the handout. Naturally curly hair was 20% in my group compared with 11% in the general population, and allergies were 35% compared with 30%. These differences do not necessarily mean the handout is wrong. A sample of 20 people is small, so one class or family group can differ from the general population simply because of chance or because the group shares similar backgrounds. This shows why sample size matters when interpreting biological data.

Overall, the investigation demonstrates that observable traits can be used to study variation in a population, but it also shows the limits of simple classroom genetics. These traits are useful for introducing inheritance patterns, yet many human characteristics are influenced by multiple genes and by environmental factors. Therefore, the project works well as an introductory model of genetics and data analysis, but it should not be treated as a complete explanation of how inheritance works in all cases.

Conclusion

My genetic traits are similar to those of others in some ways and different in others. In my own results, I had detached earlobes, could roll my tongue, was right-handed, had allergies, could see red and green, and had a straight hairline. I did not have dimples, freckles, naturally curly hair, or a cleft chin, and I did not cross my left thumb over my right when clasping my hands. Several of my traits matched the more common traits in the sample, especially right-handedness, tongue rolling, detached earlobes, and red-green color vision. Other traits I did not have, such as freckles and curly hair, were also uncommon in the survey group.

The completed survey shows that each person had a different combination of traits, even though some traits appeared much more often than others. By organizing the observations into a summary table, calculating percentages, and comparing the class or family sample with general-population frequencies, I was able to answer the inquiry question with evidence rather than guesses. Overall, this project demonstrates that inherited traits vary across groups and that comparing personal traits with population data is a useful way to understand human genetic variation.

Reflection

This project helped me understand that genetics is not only about vocabulary such as genes, alleles, dominant traits, and recessive traits. It is also about collecting reliable observations, organizing evidence, and presenting results in a clear visual form. A major strength of this investigation was that the traits were easy to observe, which made it possible to collect data quickly and compare responses across the whole group. Another strength was the bar graph, because it made trends in the survey much easier to see than a list of raw counts.

One challenge in this project is that observable classroom traits do not always follow simple one-gene inheritance patterns in real life. In addition, a sample size of only 20 people can produce results that differ from the general population. If I repeated this project, I would survey more people and use a more diverse sample so that the percentages would likely be more representative. I would also double-check each response before graphing the results. Overall, this project improved my understanding of inherited traits, population variation, and how biological data can be analyzed and communicated.
