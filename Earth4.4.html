4.4 Formation of the Solar System

The most widely accepted explanation for how our solar system formed is the nebular theory. This lesson follows the collapse of a cloud of gas and dust, the birth of the Sun, the formation of planets, and the leftovers that became moons, comets, and asteroids.

Overview

Nebular Theory of Formation

The most widely accepted theory of the formation of the solar system is the nebular theory. This theory states that the solar system formed during the gravitational collapse of a nebula, a cloud of dust and gas, about 5 billion years ago.

As the cloud collapsed, it spun faster, flattened into a disk, and separated into regions that later formed the Sun, planets, moons, and smaller bodies.

A Solar System Is Born

The early solar system began as a rotating cloud of gas and dust. Gravity pulled material inward while motion spread material into a broad spinning disk.

Video: A Solar System Is Born (YouTube)
Protosun

Birth of a Sun

The bulge at the centre became more and more condensed as matter was pulled in. This bulge was becoming a new star, our Sun. We call this still-condensing future sun a protosun.

Animation of a protosun forming at the centre of the nebula.
Protoplanets

Birth of the Inner Planets

As the Sun continued to grow, clumps of matter began to form in the area around the Sun. In the great heat, these lumps continued to condense and became planets. We call these still-condensing future planets protoplanets.

As matter condensed, the dust cleared and a hole began to form around the Sun. The inner planets became fully formed, while the outer planets were still emerging from the dust.

Temperature zones

Why Are the Planets so Diverse?

If the planets all formed in the same process, why are they all so different from each other? The answer is that each planet formed in a different part of the nebula, where temperatures were different.

Near the centre, where the Sun formed, temperatures were very high. Ices were vapourized, and light atoms such as hydrogen and helium were kept from condensing. In this region, particles of rock and metal gathered to form the small rocky terrestrial planets.

  • Mercury
  • Venus
  • Earth
  • Mars

Farther out from the Sun, ices survived and joined with other particles to form the cores of planets. At these colder temperatures, hydrogen, helium, and other gases were able to condense. These large planets became the Jovian planets.

  • Jupiter
  • Saturn
  • Uranus
  • Neptune
Key idea: Distance from the Sun affected temperature, and temperature affected which materials could condense. That is why the inner and outer planets formed so differently.

Natural satellites

Moons

A moon is a large body that orbits a planet. Most of the moons in our solar system likely formed when their parent planets formed.

One exception may be Earth's moon. Scientists think it formed when a large body hit Earth, throwing debris and molten rock into orbit. Over time, this material joined together to form our moon.

Leftover material

Comets & Asteroids

A comet is a ball of rock and ice that orbits the Sun. Comets are thought to come from a giant cloud that surrounds our solar system, known as the Oort cloud.

Video: Comets (YouTube)

Between Mars and Jupiter there is a large asteroid belt. Asteroids are small rocky bodies that orbit the Sun. They are thought to be debris that never quite became a planet, probably because of the huge gravity of Jupiter.
