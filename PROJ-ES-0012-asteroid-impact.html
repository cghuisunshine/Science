Learning goals

By completing this inquiry, I will be able to:

  • Explain how impactor size, speed, density, and entry angle affect impact energy and damage.
  • Collect and present impact data in organised tables (Parts A–C).
  • Use a calculator or published formulas to perform sample calculations (mass, kinetic energy, comparisons).
  • Interpret results to answer analysis questions using evidence from my tables.

Sample calculation: kinetic energy

Formula: mass of a sphere m = (π/6) ρ d³; kinetic energy E = ½ m v².

Example (dense rock, ρ = 3000 kg/m³, diameter d = 100 m, velocity v = 50 km/s):

m = (π6) (3000) (1003) = 1.57 × 109 kg E = 0.5 × (1.57×109) × (50,0002) = 1.96 × 1018 J

TNT equivalent (1 Mt TNT = 4.184 × 1015 J):

E ≈ 1.96×1018 4.184×1015 ≈ 4.69 × 102 Mt TNT

Note: real impacts lose some energy to atmospheric breakup; calculators estimate “impact energy” and “airburst energy” separately.

Analysis questions

Which impactor type causes the most damage?

The iron impactor causes the most damage. In Part A it produces the largest crater width at 5.097 km, the highest Richter magnitude at 7, and the largest visible fireball radius at 3.22 km. By comparison, the dense-rock impactor produces a 2.530 km crater and Richter 6, while ice and porous rock produce even smaller craters and no visible fireball. Those table values show that iron creates the strongest overall impact effects in this scenario.

What factors influence crater size?

Crater size depends on projectile diameter, density, and velocity (through kinetic energy), the impact angle, and the target material. Atmospheric breakup can reduce the effective impact energy at the ground compared with the energy before atmospheric entry.

Conclusion

This inquiry shows that even a 100 m object can produce severe regional effects, and that density is a key variable: denser projectiles carry more mass (and therefore more kinetic energy) at the same diameter and speed. Crater size, shockwave damage, earthquake shaking, and thermal effects all scale with the energy delivered to the atmosphere and ground. Finally, mapping impact zones makes the hazard concrete by connecting physics to real community infrastructure.

Limitations and improvements

  • Calculator outputs vary by model assumptions (atmospheric breakup, target type, etc.). I should capture screenshots of my exact simulator runs.
  • I should verify any “incoming object” information using official, current trackers.
  • I should refine the community map using local landmarks (schools, hospitals, bridges) and add labels.
