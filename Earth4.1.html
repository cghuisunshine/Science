Introduction

The Universe and the Solar System

The universe is a very big place! It's made up of matter, energy, and a whole lot of empty space. In this section we look at scientific theories about how the universe and our solar system came to be. We will examine the parts of the universe that we know about: galaxies, stars, planets, moons, comets, and asteroids.

We know quite a bit about space, but there’s even more that we don’t know. It helps to think of scientific knowledge as a growing, changing body of information. Looking at the history of ideas (past theories and motivations) helps us see where our knowledge came from—and where it might go next.

Big picture

Organizing the Universe

Hubble deep field photos. Reproduced with permission of NASA and Space Telescope Science Institute.

The universe is made up of all matter and space. All of the stars, planets, gases, dust, and even all energy fit within the term “universe.” It can be hard to imagine the universe and how big it is.

Video: What is the Universe? (YouTube)
Structure

Galaxies

Within the universe, there are galaxies that contain billions of stars. Around some stars, there are planets, asteroids and comets.

A galaxy contains stars, gas, and dust, all held together by gravity. Even the smallest galaxies contain hundreds of thousands of stars. The Milky Way galaxy (where we live) contains more than 100 billion stars.

Galaxy shapes:
  • Elliptical galaxies are round or oval-shaped.
  • Spiral galaxies look like pinwheels with curved arms reaching from the centre.
  • Irregular galaxies don’t fit the first two groups.
Video: Galaxies (YouTube)
Our home galaxy

The Milky Way

Have you ever looked up in the night sky and seen the Milky Way?

What you are seeing is actually just one of the spirals of our galaxy, the Milky Way.

If each galaxy contains about 100 billion stars, and the universe contains about 100 billion galaxies, then the universe contains about ten billion trillion stars:

10 000 000 000 000 000 000 000 stars

This starts to give us an idea of how big the universe is. Remember that between all of those stars is a huge amount of empty space. In fact, most of the universe consists of empty space. Another way to think about it: there are more stars in the universe than there are grains of sand on all the beaches on Earth!

Video: The Milky Way (YouTube)
Stars

Stars

The stars you see in the night sky are huge balls of gas, held together by gravity. Stars generate energy in the form of light, which is why they glow. Stars can be many different sizes, colours, and temperatures. At the centre of our solar system, our Sun is an average-sized yellow star.

Star clusters are groups of stars formed at the same time and place, and they stay together for a long time due to gravity. Patterns of stars are called constellations—ancient observers noticed these patterns and named them.

Video: Stars (YouTube)
Solar system

Solar System

Our solar system contains the Sun and the eight planets that orbit the Sun. Also in our solar system are more than 60 moons, millions of asteroids, and billions of comets.

A planet is currently defined as a body that:

  • orbits the Sun,
  • has enough mass to retain a “round” shape, and
  • dominates its neighbourhood.

The planets in order from the Sun: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

Pluto note (2006): Until 2006, Pluto was the ninth planet. In 2006, astronomers reclassified Pluto as a dwarf planet. The debate continues, as some astronomers don’t agree with that decision.

A moon orbits a planet. Asteroids are small rocky bodies revolving around the Sun (sometimes called minor planets/planetoids). A comet is a ball of rock and ice that orbits the Sun; comets can develop a glowing tail when heated by the Sun.

Measurement

Measuring the Universe

Now that we have a sense of what the universe is made up of, we can look at how big it is. You probably have an idea of the vastness of space—so what can we use to measure distances in space?

Video: Measuring the Universe (YouTube)
Key unit

A Light-Year — The Stellar Yardstick

In space, light-years are often used as a main unit of measurement. A light-year is not a measurement of time; it’s the distance that a beam of light travels in one year.

Numbers to remember:
  • Speed of light ≈ 300 000 km/s
  • Distance light travels in one year ≈ 9.46 trillion km
  • Astronomers say 1 light-year instead of writing 9 460 800 000 000 km

One light-year is huge—but in the scope of the universe it can be small. For example, the Milky Way has a diameter of about 100 000 light-years (that’s a lot of zeroes!).

Video: Light-Year (YouTube)
Distance types

Stellar Distances

Because some distances in space are so huge, astronomers use three terms to describe what kinds of distances they mean:

  • Interplanetary distances are distances between planets. These are measured in kilometres (km) or in Astronomical Units (AU).
    1 AU = distance between Earth and Sun ≈ 150 000 000 km
  • Interstellar distances are distances between stars in a galaxy.
    Measured in light-years
  • Intergalactic distances are distances between galaxies.
    Also measured in light-years
