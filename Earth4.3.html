4.3 Stars, Stars, Stars

The light given off by stars reveals their colour, temperature, composition, and life stage. In this lesson, you will explore how astronomers use light and spectra to study stars and how stars change over billions of years.

Introduction

Introduction

When you look at a star, what do you see? The light given off by a star can provide information about its characteristics. Stars can be classified based on their colour, temperature, and absolute magnitude.

Stars seem to remain constant in the night sky, but this is only because their life cycles are so long. By looking at many different stars, astronomers begin to understand the various stages in the life of a star.

Telescope image for observing stars.
Light and colour

Characteristics of Stars

Ordinary light is made up of the seven colours of the rainbow: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. Prisms can bend rays of light. When rays of light are bent, the light splits into its individual colours.

Scientists can examine light using an instrument called a spectroscope. A spectroscope splits the light that passes through it and displays a series of colours. The series of colours that is produced is called a spectrum.

Stars give off light during part of their life cycle. The colour of a star can reveal a lot about its characteristics.

A spectroscope separates light into a spectrum.
Spectral evidence

Fingerprints of the Elements

The sun gives off light because the elements in it are heated to very high temperatures. Recall that an element is a substance that is made up of only one kind of atom. Every element produces a distinct colour when it is heated.

For example, when hydrogen is heated, only a few thin bands of light are produced. This same spectrum is produced whenever hydrogen is heated. On the other hand, no other element produces the same spectrum. You can think of an element's spectrum as its fingerprint.

No two people have the same fingerprint, and no two elements have the same spectrum. This means we can identify an element by the spectrum it produces. Astronomers use spectroscopes to analyze starlight and determine what elements make up a star.

Key idea: Each element produces a unique spectrum, so the light from a star reveals what the star is made of.

Temperature

Hot and Cold Colours

Have you ever noticed that when you turn the stove on, the burner begins to glow bright red as it heats up? If you turn the heat down, the colour changes to a dull, dark red as the burner cools.

This connection between colour and temperature applies to stars as well. When you see stars in the sky, they appear whitish. However, when you look through binoculars or a telescope, their colours actually vary.

Cooler stars give off reddish light, medium hot stars emit yellowish light, and very hot stars produce bluish-violet light.

Life cycle

The Lives of Stars

Although it may appear that the stars remain constant in the sky, they are actually going through a very long life cycle. It takes billions of years for a star to go from birth to death.

By studying many stars at different stages in their life cycles, astronomers have been able to piece together the life of a star. These stages include formation in a nebula, stable periods of fusion, and eventual death as objects such as white dwarfs.

Star formation

Birth of a Star

A star is born when atoms of light elements are squeezed under enough pressure for their nuclei to undergo fusion.

The force of gravity compresses atoms in interstellar gas until the fusion reactions begin. Once fusion starts, enormous amounts of energy are released and the new star begins to shine.

Main process: Gravity pulls gas together, pressure rises, and fusion begins. That is how a star starts its life.
