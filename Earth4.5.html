4.5 The Sun's Energy

The Sun is an ordinary yellow dwarf star, but it is essential to life on Earth. This lesson explores the Sun's layers, how it produces energy, and solar activity such as sunspots, flares, prominences, and solar wind.

Overview

Introduction

Our Sun is a star. It is not a particularly large star, nor is it considered small. In fact, it is rather ordinary as far as stars go. It is a yellow dwarf star, and it is the closest star to Earth.

While the Sun may not be extraordinary among stars, it is of great importance to us on Earth. The Sun controls our climate and weather and sustains all life on Earth. Without it, we would not exist.

Scientists study the Sun carefully. From it, they have learned many things about stars in general, including their structures and the processes that drive them.

Layers

Anatomy of the Sun

The vast majority of all matter in the solar system is contained in the Sun. In fact, the Sun contains over 99.8% of the total mass of our solar system.

Video: Anatomy of the Sun (YouTube)

We can only observe the outer layers of the Sun, known as the solar atmosphere. The solar atmosphere is divided into three distinct layers: the photosphere, the chromosphere, and the corona.

  1. The photosphere.
    Image representing the visible photosphere of the Sun.

    Though the Sun is made up largely of hydrogen and helium, it also contains many of the same elements found on Earth. Because of the extremely high temperatures, these elements are in gaseous form. The visible surface is the layer where we can no longer see through the gases.

    There are two transparent layers outside the photosphere known as the chromosphere and the corona.

  2. The chromosphere.

    The chromosphere is made up of gases just outside the photosphere and is about 2 500 km thick. As you move away from the photosphere, the density decreases, but the temperature increases.

  3. The corona.
    The solar corona during an eclipse.

    The corona is the Sun's outermost layer. It extends millions of kilometres into space and is extremely hot. Most of the radiation from this region is in the form of x-rays and ultraviolet rays.

    The brightness of the photosphere usually blocks our view of the corona. During a solar eclipse, the Moon blocks out the photosphere and allows us to see it.

Fusion

The Sun's Energy Source

The energy of the Sun comes from the solar core. In this central region, nuclear fusion converts hydrogen to helium and generates extremely large amounts of energy.

Over millions of years, this energy is transferred from the solar core to the outer layers of the solar atmosphere. Once it reaches the outer layers, it is radiated into space as heat, ultraviolet rays, and x-rays.

Cycles

Sunspots

Sunspots on the photosphere of the Sun.

Some regions on the photosphere are cooler than others. These darker regions are called sunspots. Sunspots vary in size, often appear in pairs or groups, and can last from a few hours to a few months.

The number of sunspots changes in a cycle of about 11 years. This cyclical behaviour is governed by the changing magnetic fields of the Sun.

Magnetism

Solar Flares

Solar flares occur when magnetic energy is discharged from the Sun's corona. Sudden streams of protons and electrons shoot violently into space and can interfere with power and communications on Earth.

Video: Solar Flares (YouTube)
Fact banner related to solar flares.
Example: In 1989, a solar flare caused a blackout across Quebec that left residents without power for more than nine hours.
Gas loops

Solar Prominences

Solar prominence extending from the Sun.

A solar prominence is an eruption of gas from the chromosphere in the form of columns or loops. These columns can shoot straight out from the Sun, or remain suspended by magnetic fields and curve back toward the surface.

Video: Solar Prominences (YouTube)
Charged particles

Solar Wind

The solar wind is made up of charged particles called plasma, mainly electrons and protons, that escape the Sun's gravity and race across the solar system.

The solar wind can strip away atmospheres from planets with weaker protection. Earth's magnetic and gravitational fields protect our atmosphere from this constant stream.

Near the poles, interactions between solar wind and Earth's magnetic field can produce beautiful lights called auroras.
