Cell Cycle

How cells grow, copy DNA, and divide

The cell cycle is the process that cells go through as they grow and divide. Most of the time, a cell is in interphase, where it grows, carries out its normal functions, and duplicates its DNA so it's ready to divide. Interphase has three parts: G1 (growth), S (synthesis/DNA replication), and G2 (final preparation before division).

After interphase, the cell enters mitosis, where the nucleus divides into two identical sets of chromosomes. This is followed by cytokinesis, when the cell's cytoplasm splits and two new cells are formed.

The cell cycle is carefully controlled by checkpoints, which act like quality-control points. These checkpoints make sure the cell is ready to continue dividing. If something goes wrong -- like DNA damage or errors during copying -- the cell can pause to fix the problem or even self-destruct to protect the organism.

Sometimes cells don't respond to these signals and keep dividing uncontrollably. This can lead to cancer, where cells grow and spread in ways they shouldn't. Mutations in genes that control the cell cycle -- such as those that normally slow growth or repair DNA -- are one of the main causes of cancer.

Questions

1. Describe what happens in interphase. What happens in G1, S, and G2?

Interphase is when the cell grows, does its normal jobs, and copies its DNA so it can divide. In G1 the cell grows, in S it replicates (copies) its DNA, and in G2 it makes final preparations for division.

2. What is the difference between mitosis and cytokinesis?

Mitosis is when the nucleus divides into two identical sets of chromosomes. Cytokinesis happens after mitosis, when the cytoplasm splits and two new cells form.

3. What are checkpoints in the cell cycle, and what can happen if a problem is found?

Checkpoints are quality-control points that make sure the cell is ready to continue dividing. If DNA is damaged or copying errors happen, the cell can pause to repair the problem, or it can self-destruct to protect the organism.

4. Explain how uncontrolled cell division can lead to cancer.

If cells ignore cell-cycle signals and checkpoints, they can keep dividing even when they should stop. This uncontrolled growth can form cancer, and the cells may spread in ways they should not.

5. What kinds of gene mutations can cause loss of cell-cycle control?

Mutations in genes that normally slow cell growth/division or repair DNA can remove the normal "brakes" and allow cells to divide when they should not.
